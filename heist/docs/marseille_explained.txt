This guide explains **heist/marseille.py**, our final bot, line by line. heist/professor.py is the same code; only its line 4 is different. Every line number in this guide is a line number in marseille.py.

Read it in this order:
- **The one-page summary and chapters 1–2** tell the whole story in a few pages. Read them twice.
- **Chapter 3** explains the five ideas you need (the value formula, the hypotheses, the Kalman filter, learning from answers, the planner). Its small examples were computed by running the real functions.
- **Chapters 4–8** are the line-by-line walkthrough. Each grey box is a piece of the file with its line numbers. Under it, every line or group of lines is explained ("L88" means line 88).
- **Chapters 9–12** list every parameter, the version history, questions the organisers may ask (with answers) and a glossary.

Two kinds of coloured boxes:
- **SAY IT LIKE THIS**: one or two sentences you can say out loud.
- **WHY**: why we chose it, and the test that decided it.

Every chapter ends with a box for your notes, and there are blank note pages at the end.

# The bot on one page

## The ten lines to remember
- **1.** V = my hand + the board + **O**, the opponent's hand sum. O is the only real unknown (about 35 ± 8 at the start).
- **2.** We keep **216 hypotheses** about how the opponent forms its price: naive, four kinds of reader, or blend quoter, times how honest its quotes are (lam), how it reacts to my trades (tau), whether it expects me to swap (kappa), and how much edge it needs to trade (2 or 7 points).
- **3.** Each hypothesis has a Gaussian belief over (O, t), where t is how much the opponent trusts my quotes, plus a log-weight.
- **4.** Their quote → **Kalman update** (under each hypothesis the quote is a straight line in O and t). Their answer to my quote → **truncated-normal update** (it tells us an interval). Their swap → the belief shifts and widens.
- **5.** Weights add the log-probability each hypothesis gave to what happened (Bayes' rule). 10% of quotes and 6% of answers may be outliers. Hypotheses 6 log-units behind the best are dropped.
- **6.** Value: **E[V | O] = α + β·O**.
- **7.** Quote: stage 1 finds the best immediate profit (including **adverse selection**); stage 2 adds a play-out of the rest of the deal under the 6 likeliest hypotheses, through a square-root-like **utility** (like the score). Then the final quote is **70% planner + 30% safe quote** (their last quote, 1.3 towards our value).
- **8.** Answer: buy, pass or sell, compared with the same yardstick.
- **9.** Move: **never peek**. Swap at most once, **only in round 5**: our lowest card if we expect to end long, our highest if short, when the gain is above 4.
- **10.** An exact attack on the house bots (off against real teams), try/except fallbacks everywhere, nothing kept between deals, standard library only (heapq, math).

# 1. The game in one page

## Cards and the stock
- One 52-card deck. A card is worth its rank: A = 1, 2 to 10, J = 11, Q = 12, K = 13. There are four copies of every rank, so the whole deck adds up to 4 × 91 = **364** (you will see 364 in the code).
- Each player gets 5 private cards, the **hand**. Five more cards are the face-down **board**. The other 37 cards are the **stack**, used only for swaps.
- The stock settles at **V = my hand + the opponent's hand + the whole board**: 15 cards, 105 on average.

## A deal has 5 rounds
- At the start of round r, board card r − 1 is turned face up: round 1 shows none, round 5 shows four. The fifth board card is never shown before settlement.
- Each round starts with a **move**, chosen by both bots at the same time: nothing, **PEEK** (pay 3, see one random card of the opponent's hand) or **SWAP** (free, once per deal: throw one card face up, draw a fresh one from the stack).
- Then two **quotes**. The **maker** names one price p. The **taker** answers: buy up to 2 lots at p + 2, sell up to 2 lots at p − 2, or pass. Then the roles swap.
- Nobody may hold more than **10 lots** long or short; the engine cuts any trade that would break this. A maker that fails to quote is quoted at 105.

%cols 12,18,25,22,23
| Round | Board shown | Move phase | First maker | Second maker |
| 1 | 0 cards | both choose | seat 0 | seat 1 |
| 2 | 1 card | both choose | seat 1 | seat 0 |
| 3 | 2 cards | both choose | seat 0 | seat 1 |
| 4 | 3 cards | both choose | seat 1 | seat 0 |
| 5 | 4 cards | our swap happens here | seat 0 | seat 1 |

So there are 10 quotes per deal, the "slots" 0 to 9 (slot s is in round s // 2 + 1). Each bot quotes 5 times and answers 5 times.

## Settlement and score
- PnL = cash from trades + position × V − money spent on peeks.
- Every deal is played twice with the seats swapped (same cards), so the luck of the cards cancels. For each pair, p = the PnL over both plays; the score is the average of **sign(p) × √|p|**. Winning by 100 counts 10, winning by 25 counts 5, so many steady wins beat one huge win.
- A call that takes more than 50 ms is replaced by a fallback answer. Our target is far below that.

## What the bot sees: the Obs object
%cols 18,82
| Field | Meaning |
| round | 1 to 5 |
| hand | my 5 cards now (ranks 1 to 13) |
| board | the board cards face up so far |
| opp_known | opponent cards I peeked that they still hold (always empty for us: we never peek) |
| unseen | unseen[r − 1] = copies of rank r I have not seen anywhere (not in my hand, the board, the discards or my peeks) |
| position | lots I hold: + long, − short |
| cash, spent | money from my trades; money paid for peeks |
| tape | every quote so far, oldest first: (round, maker "me" or "opp", price, qty). qty is what the TAKER did: +n = bought n at price + 2, −n = sold n at price − 2, 0 = passed |
| discards | every swapped-out card: (round, who, rank) |
| peeks | every peek: (round, who); nobody learns which card was seen |

## The three methods the engine calls
- `move(obs)` returns None, "PEEK" or ("SWAP", i).
- `quote(obs)` returns a price (the engine clamps it to 0..195 and rounds to 0.01).
- `respond(obs, price)` returns −2 to +2 lots.

## A tiny example
I quote 100. The opponent buys 2 lots: it pays 102 each and I am now short 2. The final V is 110. My PnL = 2 × (102 − 110) = −16. The opponent bought because it knew its hand was high. Losing to the better-informed side like this is called **adverse selection**, and much of the code is about avoiding it.

# 2. The big picture

## The problem in one line
V = my hand (known) + the board (partly known) + **O**, the opponent's hand sum, which is the only real unknown. So the game is two things: (1) estimate O, and (2) know what the opponent believes, because that decides how it trades against my prices.

## Job 1: inference (learning)
We keep a list of 216 **hypotheses**, each a possible opponent style: does it read my quotes, how honest are its own quotes, how much edge does it need before it trades, and so on. Each hypothesis carries its own Gaussian belief about (O, t) (t = how much the opponent trusts my quotes) and a weight that says how well it has predicted the opponent so far. Every opponent quote and every answer to my quotes updates all of them.

## Job 2: decisions (planning)
For each possible action (a price to quote; buy, pass or sell) we compute the expected profit now plus a quick simulation of the rest of the deal under the 6 most likely hypotheses, score the total with a concave utility that mirrors the √ in the score, and take the best. Our final quote is then moved 30% of the way towards a **safe quote**: the opponent's last quote, moved 1.3 towards our value.

## Moves
We never peek. We swap at most once, only in round 5: our lowest card if we expect to end long, our highest if we expect to end short, when the expected gain is more than 4 points.

## Two safety layers
- An exact attack on the house bots "reader" and "sharp" (class _Reader). It switches on only when the opponent's quotes match the house formula to the cent, and off at the first surprise. Against real teams it stays off.
- Every engine call is wrapped in try/except with a simple fallback, so a bug can never crash a deal.

# 3. Five ideas, with worked examples

All numbers in this chapter come from running the functions in marseille.py.

## 3.1 The value of the stock given O (_ev, line 666)
The unknowns for me are the opponent's 5 cards (sum O) and the hidden board cards. If I knew O, the hidden board cards would be random cards from the rest of my unseen pool. So

**E[V | O] = α + β × O**, with α = my hand + visible board + (hidden board cards) × (unseen sum) / (unseen count − 5) and β = 1 − (hidden board cards) / (unseen count − 5).

Example, round 1, my hand sum 40: there are 47 unseen cards worth 364 − 40 = 324. α = 40 + 5 × 324 / 42 = 78.57 and β = 0.881. If O = 35 then V ≈ 109.4; if O = 45 then V ≈ 118.2.

Why β is below 1: if the opponent's hand is high, the cards left for the board are a little lower on average.

## 3.2 How the opponent values the stock (coefs, line 100)
From the opponent's side, my hand is the unknown. A **naive** opponent uses only its own hand and the public cards. In round 1 its fair value is 77.45 + 0.787 × O (105.0 if O = 35, 112.9 if O = 45).

A **reader** also reads my last quote q. It works out the hand my quote implies (function implied) and moves its fair value by t × (implied hand − average hand), where t is its trust in me. Example: my quote 115 implies a hand of 45, ten above average; a reader with t = 0.6 and O = 35 moves from 105 to 111.

So under every hypothesis, the opponent's fair value is a straight line in O and t:

**fair = A0 + A1·t + (B0 + B1·t)·O** (plus two small extra terms, tau·Q and kappa·ks).

That straight-line form is what makes the Kalman filter work.

## 3.3 The 216 hypotheses (_init, line 428)
%cols 18,40,24,18
| Family | Rule | Prior share | Count |
| naive | does not read my quotes | 0.30 × 0.88 | 24 |
| reader RAW | reads my last quote as it is | 0.70 × 0.55 × 0.88 | 24 |
| reader CLAMP | caps the hand my quote implies to 5..65 | 0.70 × 0.20 × 0.88 | 24 |
| reader SANE | ignores quotes implying a hand outside 12..58 | 0.70 × 0.08 × 0.88 | 24 |
| reader CUR | also reads the price it is answering | 0.70 × 0.17 × 0.88 | 24 |
| blend (BLD) | quotes a mix of its own value and my last quote | 0.12 | 96 |

Inside each family the hypotheses differ in:
- **lam**: how much of its own hand its quotes show (1, 0.7, 0.4 or 0; 0 means its quotes say nothing).
- **tau**: how much its value moves per lot I buy from it (0, 1.5 or 3; only for fully honest quoters).
- **kappa**: 0 or 3.9, its expectation that I will swap away a bad card.
- **threshold**: it needs 2 or 7 points of edge before it trades.
- blend quoters also have a **skew**: 0 or 0.9 per lot of my position.

Each hypothesis is a Python list of 13 numbers (names H_READS … H_TH, line 84): its fixed style, its belief over (O, t) as two means and a 2×2 covariance, and its log-weight.

## 3.4 Learning from an opponent quote: the Kalman filter (_see, lines 565–587)
Under a hypothesis, the opponent's quote is a straight line in (O, t) plus a little noise (SIG_Q = 0.5). For a Gaussian belief and a linear noisy reading, the **Kalman filter** is the exact Bayes update: new mean = old mean + gain × (quote − predicted quote), and the variance shrinks.

Example: an honest naive hypothesis in round 1 with belief O ~ N(35, 8^{2}). It predicts the quote 105.0. The opponent quotes 112.87, a surprise of 7.87. The gain is 1.262, so a pure Kalman step gives O ≈ 44.9 with sd 0.6: one honest quote almost reveals the hand.

We also allow a 10% chance that a quote is unexplained (EPS_Q). Here the quote is plausible, so it counts as explained with probability 0.929, and the belief becomes O ≈ 44.2 with sd 3.4. The hypothesis' log-weight changes by log(0.0281) = −3.57; hypotheses that predicted the quote better lose less weight.

## 3.5 Learning from an answer to my quote (_see, lines 588–624)
When I quote p and an opponent with take threshold th **passes**, its fair value must be between p − th and p + th. When it **buys**, its fair value is above p + th; when it **sells**, below p − th. That is an interval, not a number, so the update uses the mean and variance of a normal distribution cut to that interval (a **truncated normal**), followed by the same Kalman step.

Example (same hypothesis, I quote 100): before the answer, their fair value is believed to be 105.0 ± 6.4.
- They **pass** (fair value in 98..102): this was only 18% likely. O moves from 35 to 29.6 and its sd from 8 to 3.6.
- They **buy** (fair value above 102): 68% likely. O moves to 39.0, sd 5.7.

## 3.6 Weights and pruning (lines 587, 617, 461)
Each hypothesis' log-weight adds log(the probability it gave to what happened). This is Bayes' rule in log form. After every event, hypotheses more than 6 log-units behind the best (e^{6} = 403 times less likely) are dropped (PRUNE), and the best is reset to 0 so the numbers never underflow.

## 3.7 Choosing a price (_quote, line 907)
- **1. Their answer model.** Under each of the 6 likeliest hypotheses, the opponent's fair value when answering me is normal with mean f and sd sf.
- **2. Immediate expected profit** of quoting p (function imm): they buy nb lots with probability Φ((f − p − th) / sf), and I sell at p + 2. But they buy exactly when their hand is high, which is when V is high, so the formula subtracts β × c × φ(z), the **adverse-selection** term (c = how much O moves with their fair value).
- **3. Stage 1:** try their fair value ± 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4.5 and 6 and keep the price with the best immediate profit.
- **4. Stage 2:** for a short list of candidates (that price, their fair value, ± 3.05, ± 6.05, and extreme prices at the position limit), add a play-out of the rest of the deal (function _roll) and score the whole deal with the utility. Keep the best.
- **5. Nudge:** final quote = 0.7 × best + 0.3 × safe quote.

## 3.8 The utility (util, line 95)
util(x) = x / √(1 + |x| / 60). Small amounts count fully (util(10) = 9.3); large amounts are shrunk like a square root (util(100) = 61.2, util(400) = 144.5). The score uses sign(p)√|p| per deal pair, so we choose actions by the same shape: a sure +30 is worth more to us than a coin flip between 0 and +60, because util(30) = 24.5 is more than (0 + util(60)) / 2 = 21.2.

## 3.9 The swap (_move, line 1071)
In round 5, before the last two quotes: if we expect to end long by fpos lots, swapping our lowest card c for a random stack card (worth mstack on average) changes our PnL by about fpos × (mstack − c). Example: fpos = +6, lowest card 2, mstack 7: gain 6 × 5 = 30, more than 4, so we swap. If we expect to end short, we throw our highest card instead.

# 5. Maths helpers (lines 87–204)

Small functions used everywhere: the normal curve, the utility, the opponent's value formula, and the Kalman update.

@code 87-92 The normal curve
L87-88: Phi(x): the standard normal cumulative probability P(Z ≤ x), computed with math.erf.
L91-92: pdf(x): the standard normal density φ(x) = e^{−x²/2} / √(2π).

@code 95-97 The utility
L97: util(x) = x / √(1 + |x| / RISK). Close to x for small amounts, square-root-like for big ones, and symmetric: util(−x) = −util(x). See section 3.8.
> "We maximise a utility with the same shape as the score: small profits count fully, big ones only like a square root, so the bot prefers steady wins to gambles."

@code 100-116 The opponent's value as a straight line
st holds the public state as the opponent saw it (built by _state, line 468): bv = board sum, hb = hidden board cards, npk = number of their peeks, pk = estimated sum of my peeked cards, N = cards unknown to them, C0 = total of the cards they have not seen (still including their own hand), mo = the average unknown card if their hand is average.
L106: Unpack st.
L107: cm0 = the cards they cannot see that count in V, apart from their own hand: my unpeeked cards (5 − npk) plus the hidden board (hb).
L108: A0 = visible board + my peeked cards + cm0 cards at the average unknown value C0 / N (the O part of that average goes into B0).
L109: B0 = 1 − cm0 / N: their value rises by B0 per point of their own hand. It is below 1 because a high hand makes the unknown cards lower on average.
L110-111: No reading (naive, or I have not quoted yet): fair = A0 + B0·O.
L112-114: A raw reader adds t × [q − bv − pk − c1 × (C0 − O) / N] with c1 = 10 − npk + hb: my quote minus what a quote from an average hand would be. Written as a straight line: A1 = (q − bv − pk) − c1·C0 / N and B1 = c1 / N.
L115-116: A reader with a clamped implied hand L (5 or 65) adds t × (L − pk − (5 − npk) × the average unknown card): the clamped hand minus my average hand.
> "Every opponent style we model values the stock as a straight line in its hand sum O and its trust t. That is what lets us track it with a Kalman filter."

@code 119-120 implied
L120: The hand of mine that quote q implies, for an average opponent hand: q − board − (5 + hidden board cards) × the average unknown card. The CLAMP and SANE readers use it to spot impossible quotes.

@code 123-139 pick_q: which of my quotes a reader uses
L125-126: No quote of mine yet: nothing to read.
L127-131: SANE: the newest quote whose implied hand is between 12 and 58; if there is none, nothing.
L132: Every other rule reads my newest quote.
L133-138: CLAMP: if the quote implies more than 65 or less than 5, also return the clamp value.
L139: RAW and CUR: the newest quote, no clamp.

@code 142-154 ekf: one Kalman step (the readable version)
The belief of a hypothesis is a Gaussian over (O, t) with means (H_MO, H_MT) and covariance P = [[P00, P01], [P01, P11]]. An observation y behaves like Ho·O + Ht·t + noise; S is its predicted variance and d is how far it came out above the prediction.
L147: Read the covariance.
L148-149: P·H: how the observation moves together with O and with t.
L150-151: The Kalman gains k = P·H / S: how much to move each mean per point of surprise.
L152: g = S − Vy: how much of the observation's uncertainty was removed. For a quote all of it (Vy = 0); for an answer only part of it, because an answer only gives an interval.
L153-154: New means = old means + k·d; new covariance = P − g·k·k^{T}.
Note: ekf and mix_into are no longer called. ekf_mix (line 175) does both in one step for speed; these two stay as the readable reference.

@code 157-172 mix_into: hedging against outliers
After the update we are not sure the observation was "explained" (see EPS_Q). With probability r the new belief is right, with 1 − r the old one is. mix_into replaces this mixture of two Gaussians by one Gaussian with the same mean and covariance ("moment matching").
L161-162: The mixture means.
L163-165: The mixture covariance = E[x·x^{T}] − mean·mean^{T}. The floors 0.05 and 0.00001 keep the variances positive.
L166-167: Store the means; the trust t is kept between 0 and 1.15.
L168-172: Keep |cov(O, t)| below √(P00·P11), so the correlation stays below 1 and the matrix stays valid.

@code 175-204 ekf_mix: the same, fused (the one actually used)
The same arithmetic as ekf followed by mix_into, written out in one function so that Python does not build a tuple for every hypothesis and every observation. This was part of marseille's speed-up. Lines 178–189 are ekf; lines 190–204 are mix_into.
> "Each hypothesis keeps a Gaussian belief. Every observation moves it with a Kalman step, blended with the old belief by the chance that the observation was not an outlier."

# 6. The house-reader attack (lines 207–415)

## What this part is for
The arena had three house bots: naive, reader and sharp. The reader and the sharp value the stock with an exact formula that trusts my last quote with weight 0.8. Once we know their hand sum S exactly, we can predict every quote and answer they will make, and plan a sequence of quotes that steers their belief and then trades against it. Since 15:40 the house bots no longer count in the arena score, but this layer is harmless: it switches on only after two of their quotes fit the formula to the cent (no real team triggered it in 400 deals against 45 teams), and it switches off at the first answer that does not fit.
! It was added in nairobi (v02): +7.4 against the house reader and +10.0 against the sharp, with exactly the same decisions as before against every real team.

@code 207-221 Constants of the attack

> "The _Reader class is a special-purpose exact attack on the organisers' house bots. It only switches on when their quotes match the house formula to the cent, and it turns itself off at the first surprise, so against real teams it does nothing."

## How we tested
- **Locally**: against imitation bots of each live team, fitted to that team's public replays and calibrated so that our local scores against them match our live scores. One change at a time; a change was kept only if it was more than two standard errors better on two different sets of deals.
- **Live**: the arena plays every upload on the same deals against the same opponent uploads. Comparing two of our uploads opponent by opponent (a "paired" comparison) removes most of the luck. We kept a change only if it did not lose live, and the biggest steps (the round-5 swap and the nudge) were found and confirmed this way.
- We learned about other teams only from the public arena pages (standings, fight lists, replays). No other team's code was used.

# 11. Questions the organisers may ask

? Explain your bot in one minute.
= The stock is my hand plus the board plus the opponent's hand, so the only real unknown is the opponent's hand sum O. Our bot keeps 216 hypotheses about how the opponent forms its prices (does it read our quotes, how honest are its quotes, how much edge does it need to trade), each with a Gaussian belief about O that a Kalman filter updates from every quote and answer. To act, it scores each candidate price or answer by the expected profit now plus a quick play-out of the rest of the deal under the six likeliest hypotheses, through a square-root-like utility that matches the scoring rule. The final quote is moved 30% towards a safe price near the opponent's last quote, so we give away less information. We never peek and we swap only in round 5.

? Why use many hypotheses instead of one opponent model?
= Opponents are very different: naive bots, readers, followers, hiders. Any single model is wrong against most of them. With a weighted set, the data chooses: after a few quotes, the hypotheses that predicted the opponent well carry almost all the weight.

? What is the Kalman filter doing?
= Under each hypothesis the opponent's quote is a straight line in O (and its trust t) plus noise. For a Gaussian belief and a linear noisy reading, the Kalman filter is the exact Bayes update: move the mean by gain × surprise and shrink the variance. One honest quote can pin O down to about ±1.

? How do you learn anything from a pass?
= A pass means the opponent's value was within its threshold of my price, which is an interval. We take the mean and variance of the belief cut to that interval (a truncated normal) and apply the same Kalman step. A buy or a sell gives a one-sided interval.

? What if the opponent does something strange?
= Every observation has a small chance of being "unexplained": 10% for quotes, 6% for answers. One odd move then cannot destroy a good hypothesis, and the belief update is blended by the chance that the move was explained.

? Why 216 hypotheses, and why prune?
= Five families times honesty, trade-learning, swap expectation and threshold, plus the blend quoters, gives 216. Pruning drops those 403 times less likely than the best, which keeps every call fast (median 0.2 ms locally).

? How is a price chosen?
= In two stages. First, the price with the best immediate expected profit (including adverse selection) among offsets from the opponent's value. Then a few candidates are scored together with a play-out of the rest of the deal under the top 6 hypotheses, through the utility. Then the 30% nudge.

? What is adverse selection, and where is it in the code?
= The opponent buys from me exactly when its hand is high, which is when V is high, so a naive expected-profit formula is too optimistic. Line 960 subtracts β·c·φ(z), the exact correction when the belief is normal.

? Why a concave utility?
= The score is sign(p)·√|p| per deal pair, so big wins count less than many small ones. A utility with the same shape makes the bot prefer steady profits to big gambles. We kept RISK = 60: 120 was worse and 30 was not clearly better.

? What is the safe quote, and why 30%?
= The safe quote is the opponent's last quote, moved for new board cards and swaps, plus 1.3 towards our value. It sits inside the opponent's ±2 band, so it reveals little and is hard to trade against. Our final quote is 70% planner and 30% safe quote. Live tests: 15% gained +0.46, 30% another +0.13, nudging against every opponent another +0.06, while quoting only the safe quote lost.

? Why do you never peek?
= A peek costs 3 points and shows one random card out of five. The opponent's quotes and answers already tell us most of O, so the information is worth less than its price.

? Why swap only in round 5?
= By round 5 we know which way we are positioned, so we know which card to throw, and the thrown card is public with only two quotes left for the opponent to react. Moving the swap to round 5 (rio) gained +0.86 live.

? What does the _Reader class do? Is it still needed?
= It is an exact attack on the organisers' house bots (reader and sharp), which value the stock with a fixed formula. It switches on only when their quotes match that formula to the cent and off at the first surprise, so against real teams it never switches on. It is harmless, so we kept it.

? What happens if your code throws an error or is slow?
= Every engine method has a try/except: on an error we fall back to a card-counting value (quote), a simple edge rule (answer) or doing nothing (move). marseille does 30–40% less work than its parent; locally the median call takes 0.2 ms.

? Do you keep anything between deals?
= No. The engine creates a fresh Bot for every deal, and everything starts again from the prior.

? Why are P_EXT and P_PROBE equal to 2.0?
= They are probability thresholds, and a probability can never reach 2, so paid manipulation is switched off. It cost money against real teams (one probe quote lost 100 points in a deal in a local test) and turning it off was neutral to positive.

? What is first?
= Whether we are seat 0, which quotes first in odd rounds. We learn it at our first call: if the tape is empty when we are asked to quote, we are first.

? What are Q and tau?
= Q is the net number of lots we bought at the opponent's quotes. Some opponents raise their value when we buy from them (they conclude our hand is high); tau is how much per lot.

? What are kappa and ks?
= Some bots expect a player who is long to swap away a low card, which raises V. kappa is how much such a bot shifts its value, and ks is the direction: the sign of our position while we can still swap.

? What is the difference between lam and t?
= lam is about THEIR quotes: how much of their own hand shows in them (1 fully, 0 not at all). t is about OUR quotes: how much they believe what our quotes say about our hand.

? Why does your quote sometimes go to 0 or 195?
= Only at the position limit. At +10 lots nobody can sell to us, so a quote of 195 can only be traded in our favour (they would buy at 197), and a reader takes it as a sign of a strong hand. At −10 we quote 0 for the same reason.

? What does the play-out (_roll) assume?
= A simple deterministic future at the hypothesis' mean values: we quote 3.05 off their value when it is more than 1 away from V (so they trade our way); they trade when their edge beats their threshold; we take their quotes when V beats them by more than the 2-point spread.

? Why heapq.nlargest?
= To pick the 6 heaviest hypotheses without sorting the whole list; it is faster.

? What does rr mean in _see?
= The probability that the observation was explained by the hypothesis rather than being an outlier. It blends the updated belief with the old one.

? What are the weak spots?
= Opponents that hide their hand completely and only trade on big edges give the filter little to learn from; there we rely on the nudge and the threshold hypotheses. HAN SOLO, PaperHands and Genz7 were our hardest head-to-head matchups.

? What would you improve with more time?
= Detect the opponent's style within a deal and switch strategy per opponent: in hindsight, the best of our versions per opponent would have scored about +0.75 more than the best single version (part of that is luck), but every detector we tried misfired. More speed would also allow more hypotheses in the planner.

# 12. Glossary

%cols 24,76
| Term | Meaning |
| V | the value of the stock: the sum of both hands and the whole board |
| O | the opponent's hand sum (5 cards) |
| maker / taker | the maker names a price; the taker buys at price + 2, sells at price − 2 or passes |
| lot | one unit of the stock; at most 2 per trade, at most 10 held |
| position | lots held: + long (gains if V is high), − short |
| fair value | what a bot thinks V is worth |
| naive | an opponent that values the stock from its own cards and the public cards only |
| reader | an opponent that also reads what our quotes imply about our hand |
| blend quoter (follower) | an opponent whose quote is partly a copy of our last quote |
| hider / strategic quoter | an opponent whose quotes say nothing about its hand (lam = 0) |
| trust t | how much a reader believes our quotes |
| lam | how much of its own hand an opponent's quotes reveal |
| tau | how much an opponent's value moves per lot we buy from it |
| kappa | how much an opponent's value moves because it expects us to swap |
| take threshold | the edge an opponent needs before it trades (2 or 7 in our model) |
| hypothesis | one possible opponent style, with its own belief and weight |
| prior / posterior | belief before / after seeing the data |
| likelihood | how probable the observed move was under a hypothesis |
| log-weight | log of a hypothesis' weight; adding log-likelihoods is Bayes' rule |
| Kalman filter | the exact Bayes update of a Gaussian belief from a linear noisy reading |
| covariance | how two uncertain numbers move together (here O and t) |
| truncated normal | a normal distribution cut to an interval |
| moment matching | replacing a mixture by one Gaussian with the same mean and variance |
| outlier allowance | the EPS_Q / EPS_R chance that a move is unexplained |
| pruning | dropping hypotheses far less likely than the best |
| play-out (roll-out) | a quick simulation of the rest of the deal |
| utility | the score-like function used to compare outcomes |
| adverse selection | trading with someone who knows more: they trade exactly when it is bad for you |
| safe quote / nudge | the opponent's last quote moved 1.3 towards our value; our quote moves 30% towards it |
| mirrored deal | the same cards played twice with the seats swapped |
| paired comparison | comparing two bots on the same deals and the same opponents, so the luck cancels |
